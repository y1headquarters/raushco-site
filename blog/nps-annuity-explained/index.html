What an NPS Annuity Actually Pays You, and How

NPS forces part of your retirement corpus into a monthly pension. Here's what that money is, who sets the rate, and why it's taxed differently from your lump sum.

SR
Saket Raushan
Writes on retirement and tax planning at Raushco
July 13, 2026

If you've been contributing to the National Pension System for a few years, you've probably seen the word "annuitization" in your statement and skipped past it. Fair enough — it's not a word anyone uses in conversation. But it describes the single biggest decision NPS makes for you automatically: a slice of your retirement corpus doesn't come to you as cash. It gets converted into a monthly pension for the rest of your life. That's the annuity. Here's what it actually is, how much of your money goes into it, and why the cheque it pays you is taxed differently from everything else in your NPS account.

What "annuity" means inside NPS

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. You hand over a lump sum — in this case, part of your NPS corpus — and the insurer pays it back to you in regular installments, usually monthly, for as long as you're alive (or for as long as the specific plan you picked says). It's the opposite of a savings withdrawal. You're not drawing down a balance that eventually hits zero; you're buying a guaranteed income stream, and the insurer is the one taking on the risk of paying it for however long you happen to live.

In NPS, you don't go hunting for this contract yourself. At retirement, the corpus you've built up is split into two pieces. One piece you can take as a lump sum, largely tax-free. The other piece is compulsorily used to buy an annuity from an insurance company empanelled with the NPS system — think LIC, SBI Life, HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential Life, and a handful of others. You pick the insurer and the annuity plan from that approved list; NPS itself doesn't pay you the pension, the insurer does.

The minimum annuity rule — and why it just changed

For years, the rule was simple: at least 40% of your NPS corpus had to go into an annuity when you exited at retirement, and you could take up to 60% as a lump sum. That 60/40 split is still the number most people have heard quoted, and it's still correct for government employees under NPS.

But PFRDA amended the exit rules in December 2025, and for subscribers under the All Citizen and Corporate models — which is most private-sector and self-employed NPS subscribers — the mandatory annuity floor was lowered to 20%, with up to 80% now available as lump sum. Smaller accounts get more flexibility again: if your corpus at retirement is below a set threshold, you may be allowed to withdraw the entire amount without buying any annuity at all, on the logic that forcing someone with a modest balance into a tiny monthly pension doesn't serve them well.

The point to take away isn't the exact percentage — that's a regulatory dial PFRDA can and does move. It's that some portion of your corpus will be locked into an annuity whether you want the monthly pension or not. The rest is yours to take as cash, use for something else, or invest however you choose.

The annuity types you'll actually be choosing between

Once you know how much is going into the annuity, you still have to pick the shape of it. The insurers on the NPS panel offer a handful of standard structures, and the choice matters a lot more than people expect, because it changes both your monthly payout and what happens to the money after you die.

  • Life annuity (annuity for life): The insurer pays you a fixed monthly amount for as long as you live. When you die, the payments stop and nothing is returned to your family. Because the insurer isn't on the hook for anything beyond your lifetime, this option pays the highest monthly amount of the lot.
  • Annuity with return of purchase price (ROP): You get a fixed monthly pension for life, and when you die, the full amount you originally paid to buy the annuity goes to your nominee. It's a lower monthly payout than a plain life annuity, because the insurer is now guaranteeing a payout on death too — but it means the capital isn't simply lost.
  • Joint-life annuity with spouse: Payments continue for as long as either you or your spouse is alive. If you die first, your spouse keeps receiving the pension — usually at the same rate, sometimes at a reduced percentage, depending on the exact plan. This is the one most people with a dependent spouse actually want, even though it pays less per month than a single-life option.
  • Annuity with annual increase: The monthly payout starts lower than a flat life annuity but increases by a fixed percentage every year, to soften the effect of inflation over a long retirement. Twenty years in, this can be paying noticeably more than a flat annuity would — but you're trading a higher payout today for a lower one early on.

Several insurers also let you combine these — joint-life with return of purchase price, for instance — at a further reduction to the monthly amount. There's no universally "best" choice here; it depends on whether you have a dependent spouse, whether you want capital preserved for heirs, and how much you're relying on the annuity versus other income.

Annuity rates aren't fixed — and that's easy to miss

Here's the part that catches people off guard: NPS doesn't set the annuity rate, and neither does PFRDA. Each empanelled insurance company prices its own annuity plans, using its own actuarial assumptions about life expectancy and, critically, the prevailing interest rate environment at the time you buy. That means two things. First, the same corpus can buy meaningfully different monthly pensions depending on which insurer you pick — it's worth comparing quotes across the panel rather than defaulting to the first name you recognize. Second, and more importantly, the rate you lock in is a function of interest rates on the day you annuitize, not some number fixed years in advance. Retire into a low-rate environment and your pension will be lower for the rest of your life than if you'd retired a few years earlier or later into higher rates. There's no do-over once you've bought the annuity — the rate is locked in at purchase.

Why the annuity cheque is taxed like your salary

This is the one piece of the puzzle people are least prepared for. The lump sum you withdraw from NPS at retirement is largely tax-exempt under Section 10(12A) of the Income Tax Act. The annuity is not. Every monthly payment you receive from the insurer is added to your taxable income for that year and taxed at your applicable slab rate, exactly like salary or any other regular income.

The money going into the annuity was never taxed going in — so the tax bill was deferred, not cancelled, and it comes due on every payment you receive for the rest of your life.

It's worth sitting with that for a second when you're deciding how much to annuitize beyond the mandatory minimum, and which annuity type to pick. A larger annuity means a larger guaranteed income — and a larger recurring tax bill, year after year, for as long as the pension lasts. None of that makes annuitizing a bad idea; a predictable income for life is exactly what a lot of retirees need. It just means the "pension" figure an insurer quotes you is a gross number, and your real monthly income will be a bit less once it's taxed.
